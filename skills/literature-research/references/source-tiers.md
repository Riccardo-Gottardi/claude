# Source tiers

Grade every source before relying on it. The tier tells the reader how much weight a claim can bear. A claim central to the answer should rest on Tier 1 or 2; Tier 3 and informal sources support intuition only.

## Tier 1: peer-reviewed, well-ranked

- Papers in high-quality journals (Q1 in SJR/JCR for the field) and top conferences (CORE A*/A, or the recognised flagship venues of the field). See [venues.md](venues.md).
- Survey and review articles in those venues. They are the best entry point because they map the field and list the foundations.
- Seminal papers that introduced a method or result, even if old, as long as the venue was peer-reviewed.

## Tier 2: authoritative but not journal-ranked

- Reference textbooks and monographs from established publishers or authors (e.g. Springer, Cambridge, MIT Press, Wiley, Foundations and Trends).
- Standards and official documentation (ISO, IEEE, NIST, agency data-product manuals, library docs for a specific tool).
- Theses from reputable universities, mainly for detail not found elsewhere.
- **Preprints** (arXiv, bioRxiv, SSRN). Accept when highly cited or from established groups, and always label "preprint, not peer-reviewed". Check whether a peer-reviewed version exists and cite that instead.
- Papers in lower-ranked but legitimate peer-reviewed venues: usable, but corroborate central claims.

## Tier 3: respected secondary material

- University lecture notes and course pages from recognised institutions.
- Technical reports from established labs and organisations.
- Wikipedia and encyclopedias: good for orientation and for finding primary sources; never the only support for a claim.

## Not acceptable as evidence

- Content with no identifiable author or affiliation, SEO or content-farm articles.
- Predatory or unverifiable journals (no real peer review, pay-to-publish with no editorial standards). If in doubt, check indexing in Scopus/Web of Science/DOAJ.
- AI-generated summaries and answers presented as sources.
- Retracted papers (check the publisher page for notices).

## Signals for judging an unfamiliar source

No single signal decides; look at several.

- Venue: indexed in Scopus/Web of Science, SJR quartile, CORE rank, editorial board.
- Authors: affiliation, track record on the topic.
- Citations: count relative to age and field (a 2024 paper with 30 citations can be strong; a 2012 one with 30 is not).
- Reproducibility: code, data and methods available.
- Consistency: agrees with, or convincingly explains disagreement with, other good sources.
- Recency: matters for fast-moving fields (ML, remote sensing methods); mathematical foundations age slowly.

## Informal resources

Used in quick mode's *Further reading (informal)*, for readers who learn well from intuitive explanations. They are not evidence, so keep them clearly separate from the cited sources.

Include one only if all of these hold:

- The author is identifiable and has relevant expertise (researcher, lecturer, practitioner with a track record).
- Its content is consistent with the Tier 1-2 sources you found. Skim for errors; do not recommend something you have not opened.
- It adds something the formal sources do not: visual intuition, worked examples, interactivity, plain language.
- The link works and you can say in one line what it is best for.

Examples of the kind of resource that qualifies (illustrative, not a fixed list; check that each is still available and relevant): explorable explanations such as Seeing Theory or Victor Powell's Setosa visualisations, long-form technical blogs by researchers (e.g. Chris Olah, Lilian Weng, Jay Alammar), Distill articles, 3Blue1Brown videos, and lecture series from universities.
